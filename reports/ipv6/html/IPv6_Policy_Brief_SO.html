How to interpret the numbers in this report?
The values presented in this document are the Internet Resilience Index, normalized to 1–100. These values should be interpreted as a score rather than an absolute value. In some cases, the raw absolute values are presented to provide additional context.

Executive summary

Somalia currently faces a profound digital development paradox. While the country's foundational routing security is exceptionally high, with 100.0% RPKI coverage for its existing IPv6 prefixes, the actual deployment of IPv6 to end-users remains effectively stagnant at 0.5%. This puts Somalia in the bottom tier of global connectivity (ranked #216 of 243 countries), trailing the global average of 24.4% by a significant margin. The national ecosystem is characterized by high capability in core administrative configurations but a near-total absence of operational execution within the last-mile infrastructure of the country’s dominant telecommunications providers.

The technical landscape is defined by extreme market concentration where two "Bottleneck" providers—Hormuud Telecom (42.9% market share) and Telesom (18.4% market share)—hold the keys to national modernization. Neither provider has achieved meaningful IPv6 adoption, despite Telesom maintaining 54 prefixes in the global routing table. Furthermore, 11 of the 15 assessed ISPs (covering 36.2% of the market) are classified as "Ghost" networks, possessing no IPv6 allocations whatsoever. While the public sector shows promising signs of readiness with a 75% IPv6-capability rate among government domains, the broader commercial web ecosystem is virtually invisible on the modern internet, with 0% of popular domestic domains resolving over IPv6. Addressing these configuration and hardware bottlenecks at the top-tier ISP level represents the single most efficient path to internet resilience in Somalia.

1. Where Somalia stands

In comparison to its regional and global peers, Somalia is currently underperforming in the transition to modern internet protocols. With 0.5% adoption, Somalia sits at the same level as Cambodia (0.48%) and Lebanon (0.48%), and slightly behind neighbors like Tanzania (0.55%). The gap between Somalia and the global average (24.4%) is a substantial 23.9 percentage points, reflecting a market that has not yet prioritized the migration away from legacy IPv4 address space. Despite this, the underlying infrastructure is not the primary barrier; 100% of assessed ISPs have upstream connectivity to IPv6-capable networks, and the national Internet Exchange Point (SoIXP) is fully IPv6-enabled on its LAN.

Two readings matter for policy. First, the 100% RPKI coverage indicates that when Somali operators do deploy IPv6, they do so with a high degree of routing security, which is a rare and valuable asset. Second, the "Ghost" status of 36.2% of the market suggests that a lack of RIPE NCC membership or administrative awareness is preventing over a third of the market from even beginning the transition process.

2. Where Somalia is improving

2.1 Routing security and prefix protection

Somalia is a global leader in IPv6 routing security relative to its size. Every IPv6 prefix currently announced by Somali operators is covered by a Route Origin Authorization (ROA), ensuring that 100% of the country’s IPv6 footprint is protected against BGP hijacking. This provides a secure foundation for future growth, meaning that as adoption increases, the traffic will be inherently more secure than in many more "advanced" neighboring markets.

2.2 DNS Infrastructure and ccTLD Readiness

The .so country-code Top Level Domain (ccTLD) shows significant signs of modernization. Sampled data indicates that 51.1% of .so domains are IPv6-ready, which is actually slightly higher than the .com readiness within the same market (50.3%). Furthermore, 80.9% of the authoritative nameservers supporting the .so ecosystem are reachable via IPv6, and 66.7% of glue records are already IPv6-capable. This suggests that the registry and name-resolution layers are far ahead of the consumer access layer.

3. Where Somalia could improve

The following weaknesses are ordered by their impact on national resilience: the total absence of adoption by market leaders constitutes a 61.3% resilience gap, followed by a lack of RIR allocations for smaller ISPs and the failure of domestic networks to peer via IPv6.

3.1 High-impact ISP bottlenecks

The most critical bottleneck in Somalia is the lack of deployment by the two largest operators. Hormuud Telecom and Telesom together control over 61% of the market. Hormuud, in particular, has 0.0% adoption despite its massive 42.9% market share. Targeted intervention with these two providers would effectively solve the national adoption crisis.

3.2 Administrative "Ghost" networks

A secondary challenge is the high number of "Ghost" networks—ISPs that are active in the market but have zero IPv6 prefixes in the global routing table. This indicates a failure at the administrative level to secure IP resources from RIPE NCC or AFRINIC. Currently, 11 ISPs fall into this category, representing over 36% of Somali internet users who have no path to IPv6 connectivity regardless of their hardware.

6. Policy gaps and loopholes

6.1 Where policy is absent or unenforced

Gap Consequence Remedy

Unmonitored RIR status 36% of market lacks basic protocol resources. Link license renewals to active RIR membership.
Non-peering at SoIXP Domestic traffic takes high-latency international routes. Incentivize local IPv6 peering via licensing perks.

6.2 Loopholes to avoid creating

  • Carrier-Grade NAT (CGN) complacency: Do not allow ISPs to rely indefinitely on CGN as a substitute for IPv6; it degrades performance and increases security costs.
  • Proprietary transition silos: Discourage ISPs from using non-standard transition mechanisms that fragment the local internet.
  • Static licensing: Avoid licensing frameworks that do not require periodic updates to technical standards, which can leave Somalia stuck on legacy protocols.
  • Exempting small ISPs: While large ISPs are bottlenecks, exempting "Ghost" ISPs from RIR requirements prevents them from ever scaling securely.

Conclusion

Somalia is currently in a state of operational readiness that is not yet matched by actual traffic delivery. The country has successfully implemented high-level security (100% RPKI) and basic exchange infrastructure (IPv6-ready IXP LAN), but has failed to bridge the "last mile" to the user. The extreme concentration of the market means that Somalia does not require a thousand small changes; it requires two significant shifts by its leading operators, Hormuud and Telesom.

By sequencing these cheap administrative and configuration wins before moving to capital-heavy hardware upgrades, Somalia can rapidly vault from the bottom of global rankings to a position of regional leadership. The readiness of the .so ccTLD and the public sector demonstrates that the technical talent exists domestically; what is now required is the regulatory momentum to ensure the commercial sector follows this lead.
