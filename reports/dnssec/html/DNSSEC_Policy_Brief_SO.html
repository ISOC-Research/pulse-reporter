Executive summary

Somalia exhibits a stark divergence between its core Internet infrastructure readiness and its operational security deployment. At the authoritative level, the .so country-code Top-Level Domain (ccTLD) demonstrates robust architectural standards. The registry maintains two authoritative nameservers, both of which are fully IPv6-enabled and distributed across distinct Autonomous Systems (AS42 and AS37177). This configuration provides a resilient foundation for national domain services, achieving 100.0% IPv6 enablement at the root of the national namespace. However, this technical readiness does not translate to the eyeball network layer, where DNSSEC validation remains negligible.

Technical metrics reveal that only 2.02% of observed DNS queries in Somalia are DNSSEC-validated (SECURE), while the vast majority (97.49%) remain INSECURE. This gap is primarily driven by extreme market concentration; a single provider, Golis Telecom (AS328250), accounts for 77.49% of all observed DNS traffic but maintains a validation rate of just 0.82%. In contrast, smaller operators like Telesom (AS37473) show higher technical maturity with 14.01% validation. Furthermore, there is a total disconnect between infrastructure capability and usage: while 100% of the .so authoritative nameservers support IPv6, 0.00% of end-user DNS traffic is currently carried over IPv6. Addressing these bottlenecks requires a shift from infrastructure provisioning to operational configuration across the dominant ISP landscape.

1. Where Somalia stands

Somalia sits well below sub-regional and global averages for DNSSEC validation. While the global push toward secure DNS aims for universal validation to prevent man-in-the-middle attacks and DNS spoofing, Somalia's 2.02% secure query share places it in the earliest stages of security maturity. The country relies heavily on global TLDs, specifically .com (82.74%) and .net (12.36%), indicating that the national .so namespace currently holds minimal weight in daily digital activity for Somali users.

Two readings matter for policy. First, the lack of DNSSEC validation at the ISP level means that even if a website (like a government portal or bank) signs its zone with DNSSEC, the majority of Somali users will not benefit from that security because their service provider's resolvers are not validating the signatures. Second, the technical resilience of the .so ccTLD is currently an "idling engine"; the registry has implemented IPv6 and ASN diversity, but without a corresponding push for local domain registration and ISP-side validation, these improvements remain underutilized.

2. Where Somalia is improving

2.1 Robustness of the .so authoritative core

The management of the .so ccTLD infrastructure meets high standards for availability and protocol readiness. By utilizing two distinct Autonomous Systems for its two authoritative nameservers, the registry ensures that a routing failure or outage in one network provider does not render the entire national namespace unreachable. Furthermore, the 100% IPv6 enablement of these nameservers demonstrates that the core registry is prepared for the next generation of Internet protocols.

3. Where Somalia could improve

The primary bottleneck to Internet resilience in Somalia is the concentration of unvalidated DNS traffic within a single dominant provider, which increases the systemic vulnerability of the nation's digital users.

3.1 Market Concentration and Security Gaps

The reliance on Golis Telecom (AS328250) for nearly 78% of DNS queries creates a "single point of failure" for national security policy. Because this specific network has a DNSSEC validation rate of less than 1%, the vast majority of the country is exposed to DNS hijacking. Improving Somalia’s national security score depends almost entirely on the configuration changes made within this one network.

3.2 Protocol Lag in User Traffic

Despite 100% of .so nameservers being IPv6-ready, there is effectively zero IPv6 traffic being generated by end-users in Somalia. This indicates a failure in the transition from the core infrastructure to the "eyeball" networks. Until local ISPs begin assigning IPv6 addresses to subscribers and configuring resolvers to prefer IPv6, the resilience benefits of the protocol (such as avoiding CGNAT bottlenecks) will remain inaccessible.

6.2 Loopholes to avoid creating

  • Voluntary-only compliance: Allowing DNSSEC validation to remain "optional" for major ISPs will result in the status quo (under 3% adoption) persisting indefinitely.
  • Proprietary DNS filters: Avoid mandating national-level DNS filtering that might break DNSSEC validation chains or introduce SERVFAIL errors.
  • Infrastructure-only focus: Investing in more nameservers without addressing why users aren't using .so domains or IPv6 will result in wasted capital.
  • Centralized resolver mandates: Forcing all ISPs to use a single national resolver could create a massive bottleneck and security risk compared to distributed, validating ISP resolvers.

Conclusion

Somalia presents a classic case of "capability without deployment." The core of the .so namespace is technically sound, featuring IPv6 readiness and ASN diversity that exceeds many regional peers. However, the operational reality for Somali citizens is an insecure DNS ecosystem where nearly 98% of traffic is unprotected and 100% is trapped in legacy IPv4 protocols. The extreme concentration of traffic within Golis Telecom means that national resilience is currently dependent on the configuration choices of a single commercial entity.

The path forward does not require massive capital expenditure or new subsea cables in the immediate term. Instead, it requires a coordinated regulatory push to activate DNSSEC validation on existing ISP resolvers and a strategic effort to migrate national digital assets to the .so ccTLD. By sequencing these "quick wins"—which are largely configuration-based—Somalia can rapidly improve its security posture and build the regulatory momentum needed for the more complex task of a national IPv6 transition.
