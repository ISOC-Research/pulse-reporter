Executive summary

The peering ecosystem in Morocco presents a structural paradox where foundational infrastructure exists but remains significantly underutilized by domestic network operators. With 30 actively routed autonomous systems (ASNs) and five established data center facilities, the technical prerequisites for a robust interconnection fabric are present. However, the operational reality is characterized by a low domestic IXP penetration rate of 13.33% and a total absence of Tier-1 Content Delivery Network (CDN) presence at local exchange points. This lack of local peering forces a substantial portion of domestic traffic to be exchanged internationally, resulting in a low traffic localization ratio of 13.91%.

Technical performance metrics highlight the consequences of this interconnection deficit. The median eyeball edge latency stands at 52.21 ms, which is significantly higher than the sub-20 ms threshold typically observed in markets with localized content and mature peering. While Morocco has successfully established two operational Internet Exchange Points (IXPs)—CAS-IX in Casablanca and FEZIX in Fez—the participation is limited to just four domestic ASNs. Furthermore, none of the nine global Tier-1 CDNs have localized their infrastructure within domestic facilities or at the IXPs, creating a heavy reliance on high-latency international transit for content delivery.

1. Where Morocco stands

Morocco’s peering landscape is currently in a nascent stage compared to regional peers and global best practices. While the country has successfully moved beyond a single-monopoly gateway by authorizing 30 routed ASNs, the interconnection between these networks is not happening domestically. The 13.91% traffic localization ratio indicates that nearly 86% of traffic is likely leaving the country to be exchanged at European hubs like Marseille, Madrid, or Frankfurt, even when the destination is a domestic user. This "international tromboning" is the primary driver behind the 52.21 ms median latency.

Two readings matter for policy. First, the existence of five peering facilities suggests that the bottleneck is not physical space or power, but rather the commercial and regulatory frameworks that govern interconnection. Second, the total absence of all nine Tier-1 CDNs indicates that the "eyeball" networks in Morocco have not yet achieved the peering density or price transparency required to attract global content caches, which would otherwise provide immediate latency relief for citizens.

2. Where Morocco is improving

2.1 Operational status of regional exchange hubs

Unlike many emerging markets that struggle to launch a single IXP, Morocco has successfully operationalized two exchange points in geographically distinct cities. This provides a baseline for disaster recovery and regional resilience. Both CAS-IX and FEZIX are currently reported as operational with connected networks, ensuring that the technical platform for peering is already established without requiring new capital expenditure for basic switching infrastructure.

2.2 Automation and Route Server adoption

Among the small cohort of networks that do peer at domestic IXPs, there is a 50% utilization rate of Route Servers. This indicates that half of the peering participants are utilizing automated BGP session management rather than relying solely on bilateral agreements. This technical readiness is a positive sign for scalability; as more ASNs join the exchange, the existing Route Server infrastructure will allow them to interconnect with multiple peers through a single session, reducing the administrative burden on network engineers.

3. Where Morocco could improve

The primary weaknesses in Morocco's peering ecosystem are ordered by their impact on resilience: first, the near-total lack of domestic ASN participation in local exchanges; second, the absence of localized global content; and third, the lack of telemetry reporting which obscures traffic visibility.

3.1 Extremely low domestic IXP penetration

With only 4 out of 30 active ASNs participating at local IXPs, the vast majority of Moroccan networks remain siloed. This means that an ISP in Casablanca likely sends traffic to a local government agency via an international transit provider rather than a local cross-connect. This increases costs for operators and degrades performance for end-users.

3.2 Tier-1 CDN and content localization gap

Morocco currently hosts 0 out of 9 major global CDNs at its IXPs or within its five data center facilities. This is the single largest contributor to high latency. In a mature peering ecosystem, at least 5-7 of these providers would be present locally to serve popular video, social media, and cloud content directly from Moroccan soil.

6. Policy gaps and loopholes

6.1 Where policy is absent or unenforced

Gap Consequence Remedy
No Peering Mandate Dominant ISPs refuse to peer with smaller entrants locally. Introduce "Peer-or-Pay" frameworks for dominant ASNs.
High Cross-Connect Fees Physical connection costs exceed the value of peering. Regulate IXP cross-connect pricing to cost-recovery levels.
Cloud Localization Barriers Global CDNs avoid the market due to data residency uncertainty. Clarify data protection laws for cached non-personal content.
Lack of Neutral Facilities ISPs are forced to peer in competitor-owned data centers. Incentivize truly carrier-neutral 3rd party facilities.

6.2 Loopholes to avoid creating

  • Avoid mandatory data localized in transit: Forcing all traffic through a single government "clearinghouse" IXP will create a single point of failure and increase latency.
  • Avoid high licensing fees for IXPs: Treating an IXP as a full telecommunications carrier with expensive license fees discourages non-profit community exchanges.
  • Do not subsidize international transit: Lowering the cost of international bandwidth without incentivizing local peering will further entrench the dependency on European hubs.
  • Prevent exclusive peering: Ensure that dominant operators cannot sign exclusive peering deals with CDNs that exclude smaller ISPs from accessing local caches.

Conclusion

Morocco possesses a credible physical infrastructure with 30 active networks and five data centers, yet its interconnection maturity is lagging. The primary obstacle is not a lack of capacity, but a disconnect between network allocation and operational peering participation. By failing to peer locally, Moroccan operators are essentially paying international transit providers to carry traffic that could be exchanged for the price of a local cross-connect, while simultaneously subjecting citizens to latencies that are double the global best-practice target.

The path forward requires a transition from isolated network silos to a collaborative peering ecosystem. By executing the five identified quick wins—specifically onboarding the remaining 26 domestic ASNs and localizing at least two global CDNs—Morocco can significantly improve its Internet resilience and user experience. Sequencing these low-cost configuration and policy adjustments before pursuing capital-heavy infrastructure projects will build the regulatory and commercial momentum necessary to transform Morocco into a regional digital hub.
