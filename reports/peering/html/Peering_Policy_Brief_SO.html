Executive summary

Somalia's Internet ecosystem demonstrates a foundational presence of autonomous systems (ASNs), with 19 of 24 allocated ASNs actively routed, representing a significant 79.17% active routing ratio. However, the country's peering and interconnection landscape exhibits severe underdevelopment, posing substantial barriers to local traffic exchange, content localization, and overall Internet resilience. The sole listed Internet Exchange Point (IXP), SoIXP in Mogadishu, is currently non-operational with zero connected networks and no route server utilization. This critical gap translates directly into a 0.00% domestic IXP penetration rate among active ASNs, indicating a complete absence of local peering. Despite the presence of two peering facilities, there is no in-country presence or peering activity from any of the nine global Tier-1 Content Delivery Networks (CDNs), further exacerbating the reliance on costly international transit for locally destined traffic.

The consequences of this underdeveloped peering infrastructure are evident in key performance indicators. Somalia's traffic localization ratio stands at a critically low 1.00%, confirming that the vast majority of domestic Internet traffic must traverse international links, incurring higher costs and increased latency. This inefficiency is reflected in a median eyeball edge latency of 85.57 ms, significantly higher than regional and global best practice targets. The high geographic concentration of the single IXP in Mogadishu (HHI 1.0000) also highlights a lack of infrastructure diversity, exposing the entire national Internet fabric to single points of failure. Addressing these deficiencies requires a concerted effort to operationalize the existing IXP, attract domestic network participation, and secure local content caching, beginning with low-effort configuration and administrative actions to build momentum for strategic, capital-intensive investments.

1. Where Somalia stands

Somalia's current standing in the peering ecosystem reveals a nascent but largely unutilized infrastructure. While 79.17% of allocated ASNs are actively routed, indicating a reasonable foundation of network operators, this capability is not translated into local interconnection. Compared to a regional average for active routing ratios, Somalia's figure is competitive; however, in terms of local peering infrastructure and content localization, the country falls significantly short of continental and global benchmarks. The complete absence of domestic ASNs peering at an IXP, a 0.00% domestic IXP penetration rate, stands in stark contrast to the ISOC best practice target of over 50% and even to emerging markets where 20-30% is often a baseline. The country's traffic localization ratio of 1.00% is also dramatically lower than the >50% target for efficient local traffic exchange, highlighting a pervasive reliance on international transit.

The median eyeball edge latency of 85.57 ms further underscores the sub-optimal state of local interconnection. This figure is considerably higher than the sub-20 ms target for efficient and responsive Internet access, suggesting that local users experience significant delays due to traffic hair-pinning or distant content sources. The total lack of Tier-1 CDN presence either at the single non-operational IXP or within local data center facilities means that popular content is served from outside the country, contributing directly to the high latency and low localization. The high geographic concentration index (HHI) of 1.0000, stemming from the single IXP being located in one city, also indicates a severe lack of redundancy and resilience in the national interconnection landscape.

Two readings matter for policy. First, the 79.17% active routing ratio indicates that a substantial number of domestic networks are operational and therefore potential participants in a local peering ecosystem. This active base represents a critical resource that can be leveraged to activate the existing IXP. Second, the effectively non-existent domestic IXP penetration rate (0.00%) and the alarmingly low traffic localization ratio (1.00%) reveal the most pressing policy challenge: enabling local exchange of traffic that is currently routed internationally. Overcoming this will require a combination of technical enablement, community building, and policy incentives to make local peering the preferred and most efficient option for domestic operators.

3. Where Somalia could improve

Somalia's Internet resilience is significantly hampered by several core bottlenecks, ordering them by increasing cost to resilience: the complete lack of domestic IXP participation, the absence of local content caching, the minimal localization of traffic leading to high latency, and the concentrated nature of its existing interconnection infrastructure.

3.1 Low Domestic IXP Participation and Route Server Utilization

The most immediate and addressable weakness in Somalia's peering ecosystem is the complete lack of domestic network participation at its sole IXP, SoIXP. With 0 of 19 active domestic ASNs peering locally, the domestic IXP penetration rate stands at 0.00%. Consequently, there is no utilization of IXP route servers, which are critical for simplifying peering agreements and fostering a robust local peering environment. This situation indicates that even basic local traffic exchange is not occurring through a dedicated interconnection point, forcing all domestic Internet traffic to transit through costly and distant international upstream providers. Operationalizing the existing IXP by onboarding domestic ASNs is a fundamental first step to enable efficient local traffic exchange and build a foundation for an resilient Internet ecosystem.

3.2 Absence of Tier-1 CDN Content Localization

A critical factor contributing to Somalia's high latency and low traffic localization is the complete absence of Tier-1 Content Delivery Networks (CDNs) within the country. None of the nine global Tier-1 CDN providers are peering at the domestic IXP, nor are they present in any of the two domestic data center facilities. This means that popular content from providers such as Google, Meta, and Netflix must be fetched from servers located outside Somalia, often incurring significant latency and consuming expensive international bandwidth. Localizing this content by attracting CDNs to deploy caches and peer within Somalia would dramatically reduce latency, improve user experience, and decrease operational costs for local ISPs.

The current IXP infrastructure in Somalia is highly concentrated, with the single listed SoIXP located exclusively in Mogadishu. This results in a Geographic Concentration Index (HHI) of 1.0000, signifying absolute infrastructure concentration. 6. Policy gaps and loopholes

6.1 Where policy is absent or unenforced

Gap Consequence Remedy

Lack of explicit IXP neutrality and open access mandate Potential for a single dominant player to control interconnection or for the IXP to become a closed club, hindering broader participation. Enact regulatory provisions ensuring IXPs operate on principles of neutrality, non-discrimination, and open access for all licensed operators.
No regulatory incentives for domestic peering ISPs face no economic or regulatory pressure to peer locally, leading to continued reliance on international and often more expensive routes. Introduce incentives such as reduced regulatory fees for IXP members or preferential treatment in licensing for operators committed to local peering.
Limited enforcement of service quality standards Without enforceable quality of service (QoS) standards, there is less impetus for operators to optimize routes and reduce latency through local peering. Establish and enforce clear QoS and latency benchmarks for Internet services, encouraging operators to seek more efficient local interconnection.

6.2 Loopholes to avoid creating

  • Avoid mandatory local peering for all domestic traffic, as this can stifle competition, create artificial bottlenecks, and penalize smaller operators with limited technical capacity or financial resources to peer.
  • Do not impose direct price controls on peering services; peering should largely remain a commercial negotiation between willing parties, facilitated by an open and competitive environment, rather than a regulated utility.
  • Refrain from mandating specific technical equipment or software for IXPs or peering setups, as this can hinder innovation, increase costs, and prevent operators from adopting more efficient or suitable technologies.
  • Do not restrict the number of IXPs or facilities, as this can lead to geographic concentration, reduce redundancy, and limit competition in the interconnection market.
  • Avoid creating disincentives for international transit by over-regulating it; international connectivity remains vital, and local peering should be a complementary, efficiency-driven choice, not a forced substitute.

Conclusion

Somalia currently faces a significant disconnect between the foundational presence of active domestic autonomous systems and the operational deployment of a robust, localized peering infrastructure. While a substantial 79.17% of allocated ASNs are actively routed, the country's sole IXP remains non-operational, resulting in a 0.00% domestic IXP penetration rate and an alarmingly low 1.00% traffic localization ratio. This structural deficiency necessitates that nearly all domestic Internet traffic hairpins through international transit, contributing to a high median eyeball edge latency of 85.57 ms and leaving the country vulnerable to single points of failure due to concentrated infrastructure.

The path forward for Somalia begins with a strategic focus on inexpensive, configuration-based quick wins and administrative actions. By prioritizing efforts such as onboarding existing domestic ASNs to the SoIXP, activating its route servers, and proactively engaging Tier-1 CDNs, Somalia can rapidly improve its peering landscape without immediate significant capital outlay. These early successes will build critical regulatory momentum and demonstrate tangible benefits, paving the way for more substantial medium-term policy reforms and long-term capital-intensive investments in diversified infrastructure and local content development. A structured, phased approach, starting with the immediate activation of existing resources, is essential to transform Somalia's Internet resilience and unlock its digital potential.
