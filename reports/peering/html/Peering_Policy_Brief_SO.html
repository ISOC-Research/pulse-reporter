Executive summary

Somalia's peering ecosystem is in an nascent state, marked by a low level of domestic traffic exchange and a significant reliance on international transit for content delivery. The country's infrastructure relies on 19 active autonomous systems, which contribute to a Peering Efficiency Score of 0.0100. This score indicates that domestic networks predominantly exchange traffic internationally rather than locally, a factor that increases operational costs for Internet Service Providers (ISPs) and elevates latency for end-users. Despite the presence of a single Internet Exchange Point (IXP) and two peering facilities, their current utilization and overall impact on traffic localization remain limited.

Key metrics highlight the challenges and opportunities within Somalia's Internet infrastructure. The Localization Ratio stands at a critically low 1.00%, meaning only a minimal portion of traffic destined for domestic users originates from or is exchanged within Somalia. This condition is exacerbated by the complete absence of Tier-1 Content Delivery Networks (CDNs) within domestic facilities, forcing user requests for popular content to traverse international links, leading to a median edge latency of 101.50 ms. While Somalia exhibits excellent performance in preventing domestic-to-domestic traffic tromboning (0.0%), this positive indicator is overshadowed by the broader lack of local traffic exchange. Addressing these challenges requires a concerted effort to enhance local peering arrangements, attract content caches, and bolster the strategic utilization of existing and future IXP infrastructure.

1. Where Somalia stands

Somalia's current peering landscape demonstrates significant room for growth in establishing a robust and efficient domestic Internet ecosystem. With 19 active autonomous systems, the country operates with a relatively limited number of networks contributing to its local traffic exchange. This is reflected in a critically low Peering Efficiency Score of 0.0100, indicating that the vast majority of Internet traffic originating and terminating within Somalia still relies on expensive and latency-inducing international transit routes, rather than leveraging domestic interconnection points. The presence of only one Internet Exchange Point (IXP) and two peering facilities underscores the foundational challenge of physical infrastructure availability and utilization.

Two readings matter for policy. First, Somalia's Localization Ratio of 1.00% is a critical indicator of the underdeveloped state of domestic traffic exchange. This exceptionally low figure means that nearly all content accessed by users within Somalia is fetched from outside the country's borders, even if the source is geographically close. Second, the Median Edge Latency of 101.50 ms directly impacts user experience, reflecting the delays incurred by traffic having to traverse international links to reach content and services that could otherwise be served locally. The absence of Tier-1 CDNs in Somalia is a direct contributor to this high latency and poor localization, as popular content cannot be cached close to end-users.

2. Where Somalia is improving

The provided data primarily offers a snapshot of Somalia's current peering ecosystem and does not contain trend data to indicate specific areas of improvement over time. The available metrics reflect the present operational status.

However, a notable strength is the 0.0% reported for Tromboning / Boomerang Routing. This indicates that domestic-to-domestic traffic, when it does occur, is effectively routed and does not leave the country's borders only to return. This is a positive foundational aspect of network design that prevents unnecessary latency and reduces transit costs for the limited local traffic exchanged. Maintaining this low tromboning rate as the ecosystem matures will be crucial for future network efficiency.

3. Where Somalia could improve

Somalia's Internet resilience can be significantly enhanced by addressing critical bottlenecks related to interconnection, content localization, and market diversity. The most pressing issues involve the extremely low localization ratio, the complete absence of local content caches, and the limited physical infrastructure for peering.

3.1 Interconnection and Localization

The core challenge for Somalia's Internet ecosystem is its severely limited domestic interconnection and traffic localization. With a Localization Ratio of merely 1.00%, virtually all Internet traffic consumed by Somali users is exchanged internationally, leading to higher costs for ISPs and suboptimal user experiences. This situation is directly linked to the complete absence of Tier-1 CDNs and other major content providers within domestic facilities. Consequently, content that could be served locally must be retrieved from international sources, increasing latency and burdening international links. While Somalia has one IXP and two peering facilities, their impact on local traffic exchange is currently minimal, suggesting underutilization or insufficient network participation.

3.2 Latency and Content Delivery

The median edge latency of 101.50 ms indicates that users in Somalia experience significant delays when accessing core Internet infrastructure and content. This high latency is a direct consequence of the lack of domestic content caching and the reliance on international transit for content delivery. Without local caches for popular services like Google, Netflix, and Akamai, every user request for such content must traverse lengthy international paths, impacting the responsiveness of applications and overall user satisfaction. Improving content delivery performance is inextricably linked to enhancing domestic interconnection and attracting major content providers to deploy infrastructure locally.

6. Policy gaps and loopholes

6.1 Where policy is absent or unenforced

Gap Consequence Remedy
Lack of explicit national IXP policy Underutilization of the single IXP and fragmented peering efforts. Develop a comprehensive national IXP strategy, including support for neutrality and growth.
Absence of incentives for local content hosting/caching Continued reliance on international content sources and high latency. Introduce tax breaks, grants, or regulatory advantages for data centers and CDNs.
No clear framework for promoting local peering ISPs default to international transit due to lack of local peering mandates or encouragement. Mandate or strongly encourage participation in IXPs for licensed ISPs.
Limited regulatory support for IXP neutrality Potential for anti-competitive practices or unequal access to peering services. Enforce strict neutrality and non-discrimination principles for all IXPs.

6.2 Loopholes to avoid creating

  • Mandating domestic peering without ensuring IXP neutrality and open access policies, which could lead to anti-competitive practices by dominant players.
  • Implementing data localization policies before sufficient domestic data center infrastructure and content caching are in place, potentially increasing costs and reducing service quality.
  • Imposing strict regulations on international transit pricing without fostering a competitive domestic peering environment, which could stifle innovation and investment.
  • Creating exclusive arrangements with single content providers or data centers that limit competition and prevent other local entities from attracting content caches.

Conclusion

Somalia's Internet infrastructure presents a paradoxical situation where fundamental physical network elements are present, yet operational and security configurations required for a resilient and localized Internet remain significantly underdeveloped. While the country benefits from excellent routing integrity demonstrated by a 0.0% tromboning rate, this strength is currently overshadowed by a critically low Localization Ratio of 1.00% and a complete absence of local Tier-1 CDN caches. This forces domestic traffic to traverse international routes, leading to higher operational costs for ISPs and a compromised user experience marked by a median edge latency of 101.50 ms.

The recommended action plan prioritizes sequencing low-cost configuration and administrative wins before embarking on capital-heavy projects. By first focusing on encouraging IXP participation, developing clear policy frameworks for peering, and promoting local content hosting, Somalia can build momentum and demonstrate tangible improvements in its Peering Efficiency Score and Localization Ratio. These initial successes can then pave the way for more significant investments in attracting CDNs and expanding infrastructure, ultimately fostering a more self-reliant, cost-efficient, and user-centric Internet ecosystem for Somalia.
